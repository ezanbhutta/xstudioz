What stationery and collateral design covers

Stationery and collateral design applies a brand identity to the physical things a company hands over — business cards, letterheads, invoices, decks, signage and packaging — and supplies each one as print-ready artwork a supplier can produce without asking questions.

These are the parts of a brand someone physically holds, and they are usually the first place a brand falls apart. A website stays coherent because one person built it once. Stationery is remade constantly — a new invoice template here, a card reordered from a different printer there, a deck assembled the night before a meeting — and each of those is a chance for the identity to drift a little further from itself.

Print is also unforgiving in ways a screen is not. A colour that sat perfectly on a monitor prints flat and dark on uncoated stock. A hairline that reads at browser size closes up on press. An icon never tested at 20 mm turns into a smudge on the back of a card. None of that shows in the design file. It appears in the box that arrives from the printer, by which point five hundred of them exist.

So the work is half drawing and half specification. Laying out the card is the visible part. Choosing the stock, setting the trim, deciding whether the mark runs as a spot colour or in process, and testing how the logo behaves at its smallest printed size is what makes the card come back looking like the thing that was approved. It is where a brand identity system stops being a document and becomes an object.

How the artwork is specified

Every printed item is supplied as a press-ready PDF with bleed, crop marks, the correct colour space and fonts outlined, alongside a written specification naming trim size, stock weight, finish and any Pantone references. A printer should be able to quote and run it without sending a single question back.

Almost all of the back-and-forth between a client and a printer is caused by artwork that was exported rather than prepared. The terms below are print industry standards, not house conventions. Any supplier will recognise them, and any designer handing you files should be able to say what they set for each one.

Standard production specifications for printed artwork
Specification Standard What it does
Bleed 3 mm / 0.125 in Artwork runs past the trim so ordinary cutting variance never leaves a white sliver at the edge.
Trim size The finished dimension 85 × 55 mm is the common European card, 3.5 × 2 in the North American one. Agreed before drawing, not after.
Safe area 3–5 mm inside the trim Nothing that must survive — type, the mark, a phone number — is allowed outside it.
Crop marks Outside the bleed Fine registration lines telling the cutter exactly where the finished edge sits.
Colour space CMYK or Pantone spot CMYK for four-colour process. A spot colour when a brand colour must match across suppliers and stocks.
Resolution 300 ppi at final size The threshold for any placed photograph. Vector artwork carries no resolution and is used wherever possible.
Stock weight Measured in gsm Letterhead commonly sits near 100–120 gsm; business cards typically run 300–400 gsm, and heavier where the card is meant to be felt. Weight is felt before anything is read.
Finish Uncoated, matt, gloss, soft-touch Uncoated stock absorbs ink and prints softer and darker, so finish is a colour decision as much as a tactile one.
Fonts Outlined or embedded The printer never needs your typeface licence installed, and nothing substitutes itself silently on the way to press.

Vector artwork sits underneath all of it. If the only version of your logo is a raster file, no setting here will save it on a sign, so which files you should have been given is worth checking first. Clearspace, minimum size and one-colour use are governed by your brand guidelines, and the printed set is drawn to obey them.

Who prints it

We design and supply the artwork; we do not print. You choose the printer — local to you, or one you already use — and the files are prepared so that any competent supplier can run them without alteration.

That split is deliberate rather than a limitation. Printing is a local business. A supplier near you can put a stock sample in your hand, hold a proof to the light with you, and deliver in days; shipping cards across the world adds cost and delay for no gain.

If your printer wants something specific — a different bleed, a preflight profile, a die line of their own — send it through and the files are adjusted to match. That is a short exchange, not a new project. If you would like a second pair of eyes on the proof before it goes on press, send that too. The four stages of the work run the same way here as everywhere else in the studio, and are described in full on the process page.

Whether this is right for you

This is a good fit if:

  • You have an identity, or are commissioning one, and now need it to exist on paper.
  • You hand things to people — a card at a conference, an invoice, a printed proposal — and want them to match the website they came from.
  • Your current templates are documents that three different people have each quietly edited.
  • You are about to print something in quantity and would rather get the specification right once than reprint it.

It is a poor fit if:

  • You need artwork tomorrow for a print deadline that cannot move.
  • You want one business card and nothing around it — a printer’s own template will serve you better and cost less, and we would rather say so.
  • You have no mark and no palette yet. There is nothing to apply, and the sensible starting point is the logo or the identity around it.

We would rather tell you that up front than take a project that was never going to work. If you are not sure which of those describes you, write anyway and describe the situation.

Common questions

What stationery does a new business actually need?

Less than most suppliers will sell you. For nearly every new company the working set is a business card, a letterhead, an email signature, and one document template that covers quotes and invoices.

Everything after that — compliment slips, folders, signage, packaging — is worth designing when there is a real reason to print it, and not before. Stationery ordered ahead of a need tends to be reprinted once the need arrives in a different shape.

Do you handle printing?

No. We design and supply the artwork; you choose the printer.

That split is deliberate. Printing is a local business, and a supplier near you can put a stock sample in your hand and deliver in days rather than weeks. The files are prepared so any competent printer can run them.

What do I give my printer?

A press-ready PDF for each item, exported with bleed, crop marks, the correct colour space, and fonts outlined or embedded — plus a written specification naming the trim size, stock weight, finish and any Pantone references.

That is everything a printer needs to quote the job and run it. If they ask for something in a different form, forward the request and the files are adjusted.

Can you work with my existing brand?

Yes, provided there is something usable to build on: vector logo artwork and a defined colour and type palette.

If the mark exists only as a PNG, or the colours were never specified beyond a screenshot, that has to be resolved before anything can be printed reliably. We would say so at the brief rather than at the proof.
