What brand guidelines are

Brand guidelines are the written rules for using a brand: how the logo is placed and sized, which colour values are correct in which system, how type is set, and how the company writes. They exist so that anyone — a printer, a developer, a new hire — can produce something correct without having to ask.

They are not aesthetic preferences written down after the fact. They are the decisions already made during the identity work — this grey and not that one, this spacing, this weight of type — which otherwise live only in the designer’s head and in a folder of artwork.

A guidelines document moves them somewhere durable, which is why it is the last stage of an identity project rather than a separate product bolted on afterwards. The mark and the system around it get decided first. The document records what was decided, so the identity stops depending on whoever drew it.

What brand guidelines should include

A usable set of brand guidelines covers nine things: logo usage, clearspace and minimum size, misuse, colour in every value system you will produce in, typography and its hierarchy, layout and grid, imagery and iconography, tone of voice, and worked application examples. Anything past that is context. Anything missing from it will be improvised by whoever needs it.

  • Logo usage — primary mark, secondary lockups, icon alone, and which to use whereLogo
  • Clearspace and minimum size, in a unit derived from the mark itselfLogo
  • Misuse — the specific wrong things, shown wrongLogo
  • Colour — HEX and RGB for screen, CMYK and Pantone for print, with proportionColour
  • Typography — the typefaces, their licences, the scale, the hierarchyType
  • Layout — grid, margins, alignment, how a page gets builtLayout
  • Imagery and iconography — what a photograph looks like, how an icon is drawnImagery
  • Tone of voice — how the company writes, with rewrites rather than adjectivesVoice
  • Applications — a card, a slide, a post, a sign, each made properlyApplied

Clearspace and minimum size

Clearspace should be expressed in a unit taken from the mark itself — the height of a counter, the width of a stroke — so it scales with the logo rather than becoming meaningless at billboard size. Minimum size needs two numbers: pixels for screen, millimetres for print. State both, and state what breaks below them.

Misuse

Misuse pages work because they are visual. Show the mark stretched, recoloured, outlined, rotated, dropped on a busy photograph, crowded by its neighbours — each shown wrong, each with one line saying why. A written prohibition is easy to skim past. A picture of the mistake somebody is about to make is not.

Colour

Colour is where most guidelines quietly fail, because one hex code gets treated as the whole answer. The same colour needs a different value in every system it will be produced in, and those values are not straight conversions of each other.

Colour value systems and where each one is used
System Medium Where it is needed
HEX Screen Websites, apps, anything set in CSS. The default reference value for everything digital.
RGB Screen The same colour in the form video, presentation and office software expect.
CMYK Print Four-ink process printing. It cannot reproduce every screen colour, so say so rather than letting someone find out on press.
Pantone Print Spot ink mixed to a published standard. Specify it wherever the colour must match across suppliers and print runs.
RAL · vinyl Fabrication Paint, signage and applied vinyl carry their own reference systems. A sign shop needs a code, not a screenshot.

The chapter should also state proportion — which colour dominates, which is an accent — and which text and background pairings clear accessibility contrast requirements.

Typography needs the same discipline: the typefaces, where their licences came from, a scale with real values rather than adjectives, and a hierarchy showing headline, subhead, body and caption on one page. Layout needs the grid, the margins and the alignment rule — that is what makes two pages built by two people look related.

Tone of voice belongs in the same document. A short list of what the company sounds like and what it does not, with three or four before-and-after rewrites, beats a page of adjectives. Where applications run deep — a full social media kit, a set of printed stationery and collateral — those are separate work, referenced from here.

Brand guidelines, style guide, or brandbook

They are the same document at different scopes. A brand style guide is usually the narrow version — logo, colour, type. Brand guidelines add layout, imagery and voice. A brandbook wraps the rules in the story and positioning behind them. No body enforces the distinction, so agree what you mean before anyone quotes for it.

The distinction that matters is whether the document is a specification or a manifesto. A specification tells a supplier the minimum reproduction size in millimetres. A manifesto tells them the brand is bold, human and unafraid. The second is easier to write, which is why so many documents are only that — and only the first stops a logo being printed at eight millimetres on a folder.

Written to be used, not admired

Guidelines fail in a predictable way. Commissioned as a deliverable, they get designed to look impressive on the day they are presented — full-bleed spreads, a manifesto, a hundred and twenty pages — and then nobody opens them, because finding the minimum size takes four minutes and guessing takes none.

Length is not quality. It is the easiest thing to add and the easiest to mistake for rigour. The right length is the one at which every question a real user will ask has an answer.

So we write in plain language and build the document as a reference rather than a read — numbered sections, a contents page, headings that match the question you arrived with. Hand it to someone who was not on the project and ask for a slide and a printed card. Everything they have to ask is a missing page.

Whether you need them yet

If you are the only person who touches the brand, you need a short set rather than none. The moment work leaves your hands — to a printer, a developer, a freelancer, your first hire — the document is the only thing standing between your brand and somebody’s best guess.

The people most likely to bend a brand out of shape are not employees. They are the suppliers and contractors who receive a logo by email with nothing attached. A printer will guess at a colour. Nobody is being careless — they were not told, and the file did not say.

For a solo founder it also works as memory. Six months on you will not remember which of the three greys was correct. Short is fine at that stage — logo rules, colour values, type, one page of applications.
