What a logo actually costs, and why.

One mark, a few file formats. That same description sells for five dollars and for fifty thousand. Here is what changes across that range, and what does not.

Why the range is so wide

A logo costs anywhere from a few dollars to five figures because the price buys the thinking behind the mark. The file at the end is the cheap part. At the low end you are buying a shape. At the high end you are buying research, original drawing, testing in real applications and written ownership. The exported PNG looks much the same either way.

Search for logo pricing and you will find $5, $500 and $50,000 attached to nearly identical descriptions. A logo. A few formats. A round of changes. The gap sits entirely in what happened before anyone opened a drawing tool.

Drawing the mark is the small part. The larger part is deciding what it should be: reading the category, understanding what the company is claiming, working out which obvious ideas competitors have already taken. That work is invisible in the deliverable, which is why two files that look alike are worth different amounts.

Price alone is a poor signal in both directions. What you can judge is whether anyone can tell you why the mark looks the way it does.

The tiers, honestly described

The market sorts into five tiers. The figures below are general industry ranges, widely reported. They describe the market, and any one studio sits somewhere inside them.

Logo design price tiers across the market, with typical ranges
Tier Typical market range What the money is buying
Generators & templates Free to about $100 Assembly. A shape pulled from a shared library and set with your name.
Gig marketplaces About $5 to $100 Speed. Minutes of a person’s time, at the volume that price requires.
Mid-range freelancers Hundreds to low thousands Original drawing and a real conversation with one accountable person.
Independent studios Low four figures upward Research, argued directions, testing, the full file set and written ownership.
Agencies Five figures and above Strategy, category positioning and the structure to roll a system out at scale.

Generators and template marketplaces hand you a usable shape in minutes. Exclusivity is what you give up. The same icon library goes to everyone who typed a similar word, and the licence often says so.

Gig marketplaces put a person in the loop, and some are genuinely capable. What the price cannot buy is time. At that rate a seller can afford minutes, so what arrives is usually a typeface beside a stock icon, with the vector files held back as an upsell.

Mid-range freelancers are where original drawing begins in earnest, with someone accountable who asks questions. The system around the mark tends to be out of scope, and so does the discipline of testing it everywhere. Quality varies more here than anywhere, so look hard at the work.

Independent studios sell the reasoning as much as the artwork: research, a few genuinely different directions argued for in context, refinement, and a package you own outright. The team stays small and the work takes the time it takes.

Agencies add category strategy and the coordination needed to change every sign, uniform and interface at once. That coordination is real work. It also puts more distance between you and the people drawing.

Now the unflattering part: for some businesses the cheap end is the right answer. If you are testing an idea this weekend and might rename it by Christmas, a four-figure mark is a bad use of money. Buy the cheap thing and commission properly once the company is real. We would rather say so than take a project that was never going to earn its fee.

What the money actually buys

Strip the tier language away and every price difference reduces to six things. Each one is work that someone did or skipped.

  • Research, deep enough to avoid the shape three competitors already use.
  • Original drawing, where someone decides the exact angle of every cut and the weight of every stroke.
  • Testing: the mark reduced to a favicon, embroidered, engraved in one colour, reversed out of a photograph, then fixed wherever it fails.
  • Complete files, meaning vector masters and every export you will realistically need, named so a stranger can find the right one.
  • Written ownership: an assignment of copyright, on paper, with the source files.
  • An argument for the design, one you can repeat to a board without flinching.

The last is the most underrated. A mark you cannot defend gets relitigated until someone junior redraws it in a slide deck. How the six come together is set out on the logo design page, and the stages on the process page.

The cost of getting it wrong

The cheapest logo is rarely the cheapest decision, because the failures land outside the design budget. Rebranding is the obvious one: reprinting cards and packaging, remaking signage, replacing the mark everywhere it appears online. It costs real money and discards whatever recognition the first mark had earned. Doing it at eighteen months, because nobody drew the original in the first place, is dearer than drawing it once.

The quieter failures are technical. Fine detail disappears at favicon size, so your most-seen application is a grey smudge. A gradient collapses when the mark must be engraved or embroidered in one colour, and the supplier will approximate it for you. Neither shows in the presentation image, which is where a cheap logo looks its best.

A mark drawn with no view of the colour, typography and layout around it rarely survives contact with them. That is the argument for treating a logo as one component of a brand identity system, drawn alongside everything it has to sit beside.

The ownership trap

At the cheap end the expensive part arrives late. You find out, two years in, that you do not own the artwork.

Two things decide it. The first is the vector master, the editable original artwork. Handed only PNGs, you own pictures of a logo, and every supplier will ask you for something you cannot send them. What should arrive is listed in which logo files you should receive.

The second is the assignment. In most jurisdictions copyright stays with whoever created the work unless it is explicitly transferred in writing. Paying an invoice does not transfer it. Marketplace licences often grant use rather than ownership, which becomes a problem the day you file a trademark. Ask for the transfer before work starts, at any price. More in the FAQ.

How much you should spend

Spend in proportion to how long the mark has to last and how much rests on it. A company you might rename next quarter does not warrant a studio engagement; a company raising money, printing packaging or hiring against its name does, and will spend more replacing a weak mark than commissioning a considered one.

We do not publish a price list. Scope is fixed and quoted per project from the brief: a wordmark for a two-person consultancy and a mark plus system for a company shipping physical product are not the same work, and should not carry the same figure.

Skip the calculator. Write and describe the situation: the name, the audience, what the mark has to sit on, what happens if it is wrong. You will get back how we would approach it, what it costs and when. If the honest answer is to spend less than we would charge, we will say so.
