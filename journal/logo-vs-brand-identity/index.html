Logo vs brand identity: where the line sits.

Three words, three different things

A logo is one asset — a single mark that identifies a company — and a brand identity is the complete visual and verbal system that mark sits inside: colour, typography, layout, imagery, voice, and the written rules for using them. A brand is neither of those; it is what people actually believe about you, which the first two influence but neither one owns.

The three are used interchangeably in most briefs, and the confusion costs money in both directions. Companies buy a logo when they needed a system, then pay for the same decisions to be made again every time someone opens a document. Others buy a full identity for a business that will only ever appear in four places.

Taken one at a time, the definitions are unambiguous.

A logo

A drawing with one job: to be recognisable as yours at any size and in any medium, including one colour and half an inch wide. It does not need to explain what the company does, and marks that try usually age badly. Recognition is the function; meaning attaches later, through use.

A brand identity

A system, of which the logo is one component. The rest is everything that makes a piece of communication recognisably yours before anyone reads a word — and, more importantly, the recorded decisions that let someone who was not in the room produce it correctly.

A brand

Not a deliverable at all. It is the impression left in a person’s head after every contact they have had with you: the work you shipped, the email you answered within the hour, the invoice that was wrong. Design shapes that impression, and is often the first contact, which gives it weight. It does not control it. You can commission the first two things on this page. The third is earned, and it can be undone in an afternoon by conduct that has nothing to do with a logo.

What an identity contains that a logo does not

A logo project answers one question. An identity project answers the ten that follow it — what colour is this company, what does it sound like, what happens when the mark meets a photograph.

  • Colour palette with defined roles and accessible pairingsColour
  • Typefaces, weights, and a working type scaleTypography
  • Grid, spacing, and layout rulesLayout
  • Photography and illustration directionImagery
  • Iconography and graphic devices drawn from the markGraphic
  • Tone of voice — how the company writes and what it never saysVerbal
  • Written rules for applying all of the aboveGuidelines

None of that is decoration. Each line is a decision that will otherwise be made ad hoc by whoever is closest to the deadline. The system is described in full on the brand identity page; the document that records it, so a team can apply it without asking, is a set of brand guidelines.

Where a logo-only company breaks

It happens the same way every time. The company below is a composite, not a client:

A company is eight months old. It has a good mark, drawn properly, and nothing else. Two people build a slide deck in the same week — the founder for an investor, the first hire for a conference. Neither has anything to work from except the logo files.

The founder pulls a blue out of the mark by eye and lands two shades off. The first hire takes the template default. One sets headings in whatever came with the deck software; the other picks something with more personality. One centres everything, the other holds a strict left margin. One stretches the mark to fill a corner, because the corner looked empty.

Nothing in either deck is wrong. Side by side, they look like two companies with a similar logo.

That is the real cost, and it is not ugliness. It is that recognition never compounds. Every asset starts from zero, and the familiarity that makes a brand worth something never accumulates. Nobody in this story made a bad decision. There was no decision to make, because nobody had written one down.

How to decide which one you need

Commission a logo on its own when the mark will appear in a small number of places that you personally control. Commission a full identity when more than one person is producing material, when new touchpoints are appearing faster than you can review them, or when you are raising money and every document is being read as evidence of how the company is run.

Four questions, answered honestly, tend to settle it.

  1. How many touchpoints, counted rather than estimated. A site, a deck, an email signature and two social profiles is a list one person can hold in their head. Add packaging, signage, an app, invoices and a hiring page, and it is no longer a logo problem.
  2. How many people make things. One person is a system of a kind: it lives in their head, and it is consistent because there is only one head. Two is where drift starts.
  3. Whether you are raising. Investors read consistency as operational competence, fairly or not. A deck, a site and a data room that agree with one another say something about the company that has nothing to do with taste.
  4. How long this phase lasts. If the name, the product or the market is likely to change inside a year, buy the mark and wait. An identity built on a position you are about to abandon is money spent twice — we would rather say that now than build one.

Sequencing the two is legitimate, with one condition: the mark has to be drawn with a system in mind, or the system later gets built around an accident. That is how we approach a logo commission even when nothing else is ordered. If the question underneath yours is really about budget, what a logo actually costs sets out the market ranges.

A mark, and a promise

The line at the top of this studio’s cover is the argument in miniature: a logo is a mark, a brand is a promise. The mark is the part that can be drawn. The promise is the part that is kept, over years, in a hundred small interactions no designer is present for.

The identity is the bridge. It is what makes the mark appear often enough, and consistently enough, for the promise to attach to it at all. A mark on its own is a signature on a document nobody has read twice.

We sell both, which is reason enough to be sceptical of any argument here that recommends more work. So here is the unflattering version: a great many companies at the start need one mark drawn properly and a short, honest set of rules — not a long document. If you are between the two and cannot tell which, that is a normal place to be, and worth describing rather than guessing at.
